Most project reports suffer from the same problem: they document what happened, but they do not drive what should happen next. Stakeholders skim them, file them, and move on. The report becomes a ritual, not a tool.

Here is how to structure project reporting so it accelerates decisions instead of just documenting activity.

## The Problem with Traditional Status Reports

A typical status report looks like this:

> "Completed tasks A, B, and C. Working on D and E. No major issues. On track."

This tells stakeholders nothing they can act on. It provides comfort but no clarity. The three common failures:

1. **Activity-focused, not decision-focused**: Reports list what was done, not what needs deciding
2. **No explicit asks**: Stakeholders do not know what is expected of them
3. **Vague status indicators**: "On track" means different things to different people

## The Decision-Centric Report Structure

Every weekly report should answer five questions:

### 1. What was completed this week?
Be specific. "Finalized vendor shortlist (3 candidates)" is better than "Worked on vendor selection."

### 2. What is planned for next week?
Again, specific. "Send RFP to 3 shortlisted vendors" not "Continue vendor process."

### 3. What decisions are needed — and from whom?
This is the most important section. Example:

| Decision | Needed from | Deadline | Impact if delayed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approve vendor RFP budget | CFO | 28 May | RFP send delayed by 1 week |
| Confirm launch date | CEO | 30 May | Marketing campaign timing affected |

### 4. What risks or blockers need attention?
Use a simple RAID format:

- **Risks**: Things that might go wrong (with mitigation)
- **Assumptions**: Things we are counting on (that need validation)
- **Issues**: Things that have already gone wrong (with action plan)
- **Dependencies**: Things we need from others (with deadline)

### 5. What changed from the plan?
If timeline, scope, or budget shifted, say so explicitly. Surprises at the end of a project are almost always visible in weekly reports — if the reports are structured to surface them.

## The One-Page Rule

If your weekly report is longer than one page, it will not be read. Force brevity:

- Bullet points, not paragraphs
- Tables for decisions and RAID items
- Red/Amber/Green indicators only where they tie to a specific action
- No background or context that stakeholders already know

## Reporting Cadence That Works

| Audience | Frequency | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Core project team | Daily standup (15 min) | Tactical: who is doing what today, any blockers |
| Project sponsor / executive | Weekly (1-page report) | Decisions needed, risks, plan vs. actual |
| Steering committee | Monthly (presentation + report) | Strategic: budget, timeline, scope, major decisions |

## The Real Test

After your next report, ask each stakeholder: "What action did you take based on this report?" If they cannot answer, the report failed — regardless of how well it was written.
